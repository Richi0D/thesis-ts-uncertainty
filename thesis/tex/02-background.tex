\section{Definition and Notation}
\label{sec:definition}

What is time series forecasting? Given a known sequence of values $X_{1:T} = [x_1, \hdots, x_T]$ with $x_t \in \mathbb{R}$, the goal of time series forecasting is to predict the next $H$ future time steps $X_{T+1:T+H} = [x_{T+1}, \hdots, x_{T+H}]$, where $T$ denotes the context length and $H$ the forecast horizon. This setting describes the univariate case.

To make the separation between known context and future targets more clear, throughout this thesis the future targets are denoted as $Y_{1:H} = [y_{1}, \hdots, y_{H}]$ with $y_h \in \mathbb{R}$ and the final model output is denoted as $\hat{Y}_{1:H} = [\hat{y}_{1}, \hdots, \hat{y}_{H}]$ with $\hat{y}_h \in \mathbb{R}$.

Modern time series models such as Chronos-2 (\citeauthor{chronos2}~\cite{chronos2}), Toto 2.0 (\citeauthor{toto2}~\cite{toto2}) and TiRex-2 (\citeauthor{tirex2}~\cite{tirex2}) extend the univariate case to multivariate and incorporate additional information by using one or multiple covariates for the historic context and forecast horizon. Following the notation of \citeauthor{tirex2}~\cite{tirex2} with minor adaptations, the input now consists of the target series $X^{tgt}_{1:T} \in \mathbb{R}^{V^{tgt} \times T}$, past covariates $X^{pcov}_{1:T} \in \mathbb{R}^{V^{pcov} \times T}$ which are observed up to time $T$, and future covariates $X^{fcov}_{1:T+H} \in \mathbb{R}^{V^{fcov} \times (T+H)}$ which are also known over the forecast horizon. The total number of variates is $V = V^{tgt} + V^{pcov} + V^{fcov}$. The predictions $Y_{1:H} \in \mathbb{R}^{V^{tgt} \times H}$ also gain an additional variate dimension.

While classic approaches such as ARIMA (\citeauthor{arima}~\cite{arima}) primarily produce point forecasts, recent models learn to approximate the conditional distribution over future outcomes:

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{P} \left(Y_{1:H} | X^{tgt}_{1:T}, X^{pcov}_{1:T}, X^{fcov}_{1:T+H} \right)
\end{equation}

This distribution is often approximated by $K$ quantile levels $\mathcal{Q} = \left\{\tau_1, \hdots, \tau_K \right\} \subset (0,1)$ learned by a parametrised function $g_{\theta}$:

\begin{equation}
	\left\{\mathcal{Q}_\tau \left(Y_{1:H} | X^{tgt}_{1:T}, X^{pcov}_{1:T}, X^{fcov}_{1:T+H} \right)\right\}_{\tau \in \mathcal{Q}} \approx g_{\theta} \left( X^{tgt}_{1:T}, X^{pcov}_{1:T}, X^{fcov}_{1:T+H}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Q}_\tau (\cdot | \cdot)$ defines the conditional $\tau$-quantile.

Further, in recent models an input sequence is often splitted to non-overlapping patches of length $P$. After extending all series over the forecast horizon, this transformation reshapes a time series $X \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times T}$ into $X \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times N \times P}$, where $N = \lceil (T + H) / P \rceil$ denotes the number of patches. This applies to targets, past covariates and future covariates. 
Usually, this patches are further embedded into a higher dimension $D$ to serve as input to the model. As a result of patching and embedding the model input has the shape $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times N \times D}$. The final hidden state is then transformed to quantile predictions $\hat{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{V^{tgt} \times H \times K}$.

To complete the notation, $B$ denotes the batch size. Since this notations describe a general form of modern time series models and of the forecasting process, individual models may deviate slightly from it. Such differences are described in the respective sections.

\begin{itemize}
	\item $X$: model input (split into $X^{tgt}$, $X^{pcov}$, $X^{fcov}$)
	\item $Y$: future targets; $\hat{Y}$: model output
	\item $Z$: embedding output; $\hat{Z}$: hidden state or intermediate model output 
	\item $T$: context length
	\item $H$: forecast horizon
	\item $V$: number of variates ($V^{tgt}$, $V^{pcov}$, $V^{fcov}$)
	\item $P$: patch size
	\item $N$: number of patches
	\item $K$: number of quantiles ($\tau \in \mathcal{Q}$)
	\item $D$: model dimension
	\item $B$: batch size
\end{itemize}

This chapter reviews the development of time series forecasting from classical statistical methods to recent foundation models. These approaches can be divided into three main categories. (I) Local models such as ARIMA (\citeauthor{arima}~\cite{arima}) and exponential smoothing (\citeauthor{ets}~\cite{ets}) fit a separate set of parameters for each time series. (II) Dataset-specific global models such as DeepAR (\citeauthor{deepar}~\cite{deepar}) and N-BEATS (\citeauthor{nbeats}~\cite{nbeats}) share their parameters across all series of a dataset, but have to be retrained for every new dataset or task. (III) Zero-shot foundation models such as Chronos (\citeauthor{chronos}~\cite{chronos}), TiRex (\citeauthor{tirex1}~\cite{tirex1}) and Toto (\citeauthor{toto1}~\cite{toto1}) follow the paradigm of large language models. They are trained on a big set of data with various time series of different domains and generate forecasts for unseen sequences without requiring additional training or fine-tuning. This chapter also covers design choices which are implemented by this category of models, such as probabilistic forecasting with quantiles, patch-based input representations, learning from additional covariates and efficient streaming.


\subsection{Classical Statistical Forecasting}
\label{sec:rw_classical}

Classical forecasting methods model each time series individually. ARIMA (\citeauthor{arima}~\cite{arima}) describes a series as a linear combination of its own past values and past forecast errors, after differencing to remove trends. While the classical Box-Jenkins methodology selects the model manually, Auto-ARIMA (\citeauthor{autoarima}~\cite{autoarima}) automates this selection. Exponential smoothing (\citeauthor{ets}~\cite{ets}) decompose a series into error, trend and seasonal components (state space form labeled as ETS) that are updated recursively. The Theta method (\citeauthor{theta}~\cite{theta}) decomposes a series into two $\theta$-lines: a linear trend line that is extrapolated directly and a line with amplified local curvature that is forecast with simple exponential smoothing. The final forecast is the average of both.

Although deep learning models often outperform the classical approaches, they remain a strong baseline in current benchmarks. They are easy to interpret and are computationally cheap. But they are limited to single series since each time series needs its own fitting step and no knowledge is transferred between different series.   


\subsection{Deep Learning and Global Models}
\label{sec:rw_deep}

A key shift was the move from local to global models, which share one set of parameters across multiple time series. Global models learn patterns that are shared across series and can therefore forecast series with little history. One enabler were, recurrent neural networks, specially the LSTM (\citeauthor{lstm}~\cite{lstm}), were among the first deep architectures applied to forecasting. They process a series step by step while maintaining a hidden state. DeepAR (\citeauthor{deepar}~\cite{deepar}) trains an autoregressive LSTM on a dataset and predicts the parameters of a likelihood at each step, from which forecasts are obtained by sampling. N-BEATS (\citeauthor{nbeats}~\cite{nbeats}) showed that a deep architecture of fully connected layers with residual connections can outperform statistical methods. N-HiTS (\citeauthor{nhits}~\cite{nhits}) extended this design with multi-rate sampling and hierarchical interpolation to improve long-horizon forecasting. 


\subsection{Transformers}
\label{sec:rw_transformers}

Due to the success of the Transformer (\citeauthor{transformerbase}~\cite{transformerbase}) in natural language processing, the architecture was soon adapted to time series forecasting. Unlike recurrent networks, self-attention connects every pair of positions directly, which promises to capture long-range dependencies without the step-by-step propagation of a hidden state. The first adaptations, however, used each time step as one token, typically embedding the values of all variates at that time step together with timestamp features. Since the cost of self-attention grows quadratically with the number of tokens, long contexts and horizons quickly became expensive.

The Temporal Fusion Transformer (\citeauthor{temporalfusiontransformers}~\cite{temporalfusiontransformers}) combines recurrence with attention. It expands the input space by adding static covariates, past-observed inputs that are only available up to the forecast origin, and known future inputs that are available over the horizon. The model encode past inputs and decode future inputs via a LSTM layer and adds a temporal self-attention layer. Informer (\citeauthor{informer}~\cite{informer}) restricted attention to the most informative queries (ProbSparse attention) and replaced autoregressive decoding with a decoder that predicts the whole horizon in a single forward pass, avoiding error accumulation over long horizons. Autoformer (\citeauthor{autoformer}~\cite{autoformer}) integrated a decomposition into trend and seasonal components into the architecture. FEDformer (\citeauthor{fedformer}~\cite{fedformer}) moved attention into the frequency domain and operated on a small subset of Fourier modes, reducing the complexity to linear in the sequence length. Their effectiveness was questioned by \citeauthor{dlinear}~\cite{dlinear}, who showed that a simple linear model can outperform them on common long-horizon benchmarks. This raised the question of whether attention over single time steps is a suitable representation for time series at all.


\subsection{Patching}
\label{sec:rw_patching}

PatchTST (\citeauthor{patchtst}~\cite{patchtst}) addressed the issue of attention for single time steps by splitting each series into patches of length $P$ and treating every patch as one token. Patching reduces the sequence length from $T$ to $N \approx T/P$, which lowers the cost of attention, and gives each token a local context of several time steps that carries more information than a single value. Patching has since become the standard input representation of time series foundation models. In addition, PatchTST processes every variate independently with shared weights (channel independence), which proved more robust than mixing variates naively. But channel independence, ignores information shared between variates. Crossformer (\citeauthor{crossformer}~\cite{crossformer}) embeds each variate patch-wise and alternates between attention over time and attention over variates.


\subsection{Probabilistic Forecasting}
\label{sec:rw_quantiles}

Point forecasts do not express how uncertain a prediction is, which is essential for decision making and for downstream tasks such as anomaly detection. Quantile regression addresses this by estimating conditional quantiles directly. The $\tau$-quantile is the value that the future observation falls below with probability $\tau$, so for example the $0.1$- and $0.9$-quantiles together span an $80\%$ prediction interval. For a single target series, the quantile loss over the forecast horizon is
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{K \cdot H} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{Q}}
	\begin{cases}
		\tau \left(y_h - \hat{y}_h^{\tau}\right) & \text{if } \hat{y}_h^{\tau} \le y_h, \\
		(1 - \tau) \left(\hat{y}_h^{\tau} - y_h\right) & \text{otherwise,}
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:quantile_loss}
\end{equation}
where $y_h$ is the observed value at horizon step $h$ and $\hat{y}_h^{\tau}$ the predicted $\tau$-quantile. The loss penalises under-prediction with weight $\tau$ and over-prediction with weight $1 - \tau$, so that its minimiser is the $\tau$-quantile of the conditional distribution. For $\tau = 0.5$, it reduces to half the absolute error and yields the median.

A model predicts one value per quantile level in a fixed set $\mathcal{Q}$ with $K = |\mathcal{Q}|$ levels, and the choice of this set differs between models. The Temporal Fusion Transformer (\citeauthor{temporalfusiontransformers}~\cite{temporalfusiontransformers}) uses $K = 3$ levels $\{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}$, while TiRex-1 (\citeauthor{tirex1}~\cite{tirex1}) and TiRex-2 (\citeauthor{tirex2}~\cite{tirex2}) use $K = 9$ levels $\{0.1, 0.2, \hdots, 0.9\}$. Chronos-2 (\citeauthor{chronos2}~\cite{chronos2}) extends this to $K = 21$ levels, $\{0.01, 0.99\}$ together with $\{0.05, 0.10, \hdots, 0.95\}$ in steps of $0.05$, to better capture the tails of the distribution and thus rare events.


\subsection{Time Series Foundation Models}
\label{sec:rw_foundation}

Time series foundation models commonly use the zero-shot forecast paradigm. This means, the model is pretrained on large and diverse collections of real and synthetic series and predict unseen series, without any training or fine-tuning on the target data. TimeGPT (\citeauthor{timegpt}~\cite{timegpt}) and Lag-Llama (\citeauthor{lagllama}~\cite{lagllama}) were early examples of this approach. Chronos (\citeauthor{chronos}~\cite{chronos}) scales and quantises time series values into a fixed vocabulary and trains a T5 language model with a cross-entropy loss, treating forecasting as next-token prediction. TimesFM (\citeauthor{timesfm}~\cite{timesfm}) uses a decoder-only Transformer over patches.

TiRex (\citeauthor{tirex1}~\cite{tirex1}) breaks apart from the Transformer and builds on the xLSTM architecture (\citeauthor{xlstm}~\cite{xlstm}). It mention that the state tracking of recurrent models is beneficial for forecasting and introduces contiguous patch masking during training to improve long-horizon predictions. Toto (\citeauthor{toto}~\cite{toto}) is a decoder-only model trained with a strong focus on observability data and uses a Student-T mixture model head instead of the common quantile head. 

Most of these first-generation foundation models are univariate: each series is forecast in isolation, and covariates cannot be used or only through additional mechanisms. 


\subsection{Multivariate Foundation Models with covariates}
\label{sec:rw_multivariate}

The recent generation of foundation models supports multivariate forecasting and covariates natively. The Chronos-2 (\citeauthor{chronos2}~\cite{chronos2}) transformer stack adds a time attention layer which aggregates information across patches within a single time series and additionally a group attention layer which aggregates information across all series within a group at each patch.
TiRex-2 (\citeauthor{tirex2}~\cite{tirex2}) combines a bidirectional xLSTM time mixer with attention over variates at each patch position. The forward recurrence processes all variates, while a reverse recurrence runs only over the known future covariates, so that information about the forecast horizon can flow backwards into the context.

These models follow the general structure introduced in Section~\ref{sec:definition}: patched inputs with targets, past covariates and future covariates stacked along the variate axis, alternating mixing along the time and variate axes, and a quantile output head. They differ, however, in the type of time mixer, in how cross-variate information is exchanged, and in how future covariates are processed.


\subsection{Efficiency and Streaming}
\label{sec:rw_recurrent}

While Transformers dominate sequence modelling, their attention cost grows quadratically with the sequence length, and autoregressive inference requires caching keys and values for the whole context. This has renewed interest in architectures with a constant-size state. 

xLSTM (\citeauthor{xlstm}~\cite{xlstm}) revisits the LSTM with exponential gating and modified memory structures. Its sLSTM variant keeps a scalar memory with recurrent connections between hidden states, which enables state tracking but requires sequential computation. The mLSTM variant replaces the scalar memory with a matrix memory, so it can be trained in parallel like linear attention. Both variants can be evaluated step by step, so that processing a new input costs $\mathcal{O}(1)$ with respect to the length of the context. TiRex and TiRex-2 build on these cells.

In applications, such as monitoring, observations arrive continuously and forecasts must be updated whenever new data becomes available. For unidirectional recurrent models, streaming inference is straightforward, since the state after the last observation summarises the whole context and only the new inputs have to be processed. For a Transformer without caching, every update requires recomputing the forward pass over the full context. This makes models, such as Tirex-2 (\citeauthor{tirex2}~\cite{tirex2}) a better fit for this applications. 


\subsection{Benchmarks}
\label{sec:rw_benchmarks}

Zero-shot forecasting models are evaluated on collections of datasets that were not used for training. Common benchmarks in this field are GIFT-Eval (\citeauthor{gifteval}~\cite{gifteval}) and fev-bench (\citeauthor{fevbench}~\cite{fevbench}). fev-bench in particular includes tasks with covariates.

A complementary approach uses synthetic data from known dynamical systems. dysts (\citeauthor{dysts}~\cite{dysts}) is a collection of over 130 chaotic systems with published parameters, together with tools to generate trajectories of arbitrary length. \citeauthor{zhang2025chaos}~\cite{zhang2025chaos} used dysts to evaluate a time series foundation model zero-shot on chaotic systems. Besides pointwise error, they measured how long forecasts stay accurate.
